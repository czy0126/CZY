Low-Profile Direct Power Converter: 350A/48V-1V 
with Planar Matrix Transformer using standard PCB 
and commercial cores  
Alejandro Figueroa, Pablo Mazariegos, Javier Goicoechea, Alejandro Castro, and José A. Cobos 
Differential Power S.L. 
28042 Madrid, Spain 
email: {alex.figueroa, pablo.mazariegos, javier.goicoechea, alejandro.castro, jcobos}@differentialpower.com 
Abstract—The 48V-1V Low-Profile Direct Power Converter, 
described in this paper, is a single-stage converter for high-
current applications with height limitation. Based on the DPx 
converter, this 5mm-height power converter takes steps 
forwards by using standard PCB and a Planar Matrix 
Transformer with commercial cores. This Low-Profile 
converter becomes a so-called electric center, worsening the 
conversion performance but improving the manufacturability. 
A non-optimized prototype was built and tested up to 350A 
output current, reaching a peak efficiency of 94% and a full-
load efficiency of 85% with 412 W/in3 power density. The 5mm 
height power converter presented in this article enables a 
power-supply-in-package for applications where the height 
dimension becomes highly restrictive. 
Keywords—Low-Profile Direct Power Converter, standard 
PCB, Planar-Matrix transformer, commercial cores, electric 
center 
I. INTRODUCTION  
Nowadays the world is living in a constant technological 
revolution in which the need to store and process information 
is becoming even more relevant. The ongoing advancement of 
power electronics is driven by both the continual evolution of 
modern technologies and the evolving demands of high-
current applications. Important enhancements have been 
developed over the last decades for providing high-
performance power conversions due to the need to minimize 
losses in power distribution networks. 
There is an emerging trend in data center power delivery 
based on feeding server racks with higher voltage. The bus 
voltage traditionally fixed at 12V was increased up to 48V, 
not only for taking advantage of existing telecommunications 
power systems but also for improving the power delivery 
capability of data centers and reduce power losses. Due to the 
different possibilities in power conversion shown in Fig. 1, 
different strategies can be implemented to achieve the 48V-
1V conversion ratio. 
 
Fig.  1.   Power conversion strategies used to power data center racks. 
The major improvement towards high-performance power 
conversions is related with power distribution structures, to 
reduce the distance between the power converter and the chip. 
This distance, known as the “last inch”, comprises the final 
millimeters that connect the power plane of the converter with 
the chip socket. For currents of thousands of amperes this 
resistance is considerably high, leading to higher conduction 
losses and voltage drops. Moreover, the chips may demand 
fast high peak currents that the power converter must be able 
to satisfy without generating unwanted transients due to 
intrinsic parasitic inductances on the power path. Nowadays, 
Lateral Power Delivery (LPD) is the main power distribution 
structure, but other power structures, such as Vertical Power 
Delivery (VPD) lead the way in future high-current 
applications. Compared to the LPD, [1] reports that in the 
VPD the conduction resistance and the parasitic inductances 
are even lower. Also, the fact that no copper tracks or pins are 
needed reduces the conduction losses to the minimum, even 
with extremely high current demands. 
Therefore, apart from having power converters with very 
high efficiency, it is also necessary to adapt the packaging 
designs of the converters to the new power delivery structures 
in order to reduce size and conduction losses by minimizing 
the current path. For that reason, it will be indispensable to 
have converters with low profiles that can be incorporated 
close to the chip without increasing the network overall size. 
II. LOW-PROFILE DIRECT POWER CONVERTER 
The Low-Profile Power Converter is based on the Direct 
Power Converter (DPx) [2], which resembles a Flyback 
converter, with the distinction that the transformer terminals 
are reversed, mirroring the configuration found in a Forward 
converter [3]. For achieving a converter height of 5mm a 
different perspective was carried out to design a new low-
profile converter for high-current applications. 
A. Power Topology  
Since the topology is transformer-based, the converter is 
not gain limited, as it is possible to change the conversion gain 
only by modifying the turns ratio. This is essential to achieve 
high conversion ratios without increasing the size and losses 
of the converter.  
For this 48V-1V power converter it is necessary to use a 
matrix structure based on six commercial magnetic cores with 
a 4:1 ratio in each of them. Moreover, the implementation of 
the partial winding in the transformer structure results in 
twelve secondaries connected in parallel (Fig. 2). The 
converter output voltage only depends on the transformer 
turns ratio (n), so it will need a previous or later stage in charge 
of the regulation.

Hence, to optimize the power converter, it is necessary to 
assess multiple power switches with varying voltage ratings 
and their respective on-resistance values. This evaluation is 
conducted to identify the switch that minimizes losses within 
the constraints of the available physical space and specified 
electrical requirements. 
Due to the mode of operation, Zero Voltage Switching 
(ZVS) and Zero Current Switching (ZCS) is possible to be 
accomplished (Fig. 3). ZVS can be achieved in both ports of 
the converter, while ZCS can be implemented in the 
secondary-side by delaying its control signal with respect to 
the primary control signal.

B. Operation fundamentals  
The equivalent model of the power converter is made up 
only of a transformer and three switches: two in the primary-
side making a half bridge connection and another switch in the 
secondary-side. The term "Direct Power Converter" is 
assigned due to the direct transfer of power when both main 
switches are closed, whereas the transformer undergoes 
demagnetization when the active clamp is closed and both 
main switches are opened.  
Throughout the conduction period, as illustrated in Fig. 4, 
the converter transmits power from the input to the output, 
with the main switches conducting simultaneously. Indeed, a 
short-circuit is created during on-time, but the parasitic 
elements in the transformer, such as the leakage inductance, 
serve to characterize the current waveform. Additionally, the 
output capacitance and load characteristics influence the shape 
of the output current waveform (Fig 5). 
 
Fig.  4.   Equivalent circuit during turn-on. 
 
Fig.  5.   Output current waveforms for different equivalent leakage 
inductances in secondary-side with an output current of 300A. 
During the turn-off period, the magnetic cores are 
demagnetized. This feature is achieved by opening the main 
switches on both sides of the transformer and closing the 
active clamp switch. 
III. TRANSFORMER STRUCTURE 
The transformer structure, made by commercial cores, is 
based on a matrix transformer with partial turns. Using these 
concepts, higher gains are achieved with a lower number of 
turns, thus reducing height and conduction losses of the 
converter. Moreover, in order to facilitate manufacturing and 
achieve higher power density the transformer winding is 
implemented on PCB [4], known as planar transformer.

For this application Ferroxcube E-cores (E14/3.5/5) and 
PLT-cores (PLT14/5/1.5) of 3C95 material are used due to 
their reduced size and shape. The magnetic core set (Fig. 7) 
can be formed either by a pair of E-cores or by a combination 
of E/PLT, depending on the desired height of the converter. 
 
Fig.  6.   Core dimensions for E (left) and PLT (right) [5]. 
Matrix transformer, patented in [6], is defined as the 
division of the main transformer into several interdependent 
transformers. In Fig. 7 it is shown how the primary-side 
winding is connected in series, while the connection of the 
secondary-side winding is in parallel. Note that the output 
capacitor and the secondary switch are part of the secondary 
winding, so they should be as close as possible to the core so 
that the termination connection is the smallest possible.  
Secondary turns are connected in parallel to reduce 
conduction losses by spreading the current into several 
synchronous rectifiers [7]. The highest conduction losses 
occur on the secondary-side, where the highest currents are 
handled. Using this transformer structure, the Low-Profile 
Direct Power Converter becomes a so-called electrical center, 
which worsens conversion performance but improves 
manufacturability.  
 
Fig.  7.   Planar Matrix Transformer and partial turns concept applied to   
the Low-Profile Direct Power Converter. 
The use of these magnetics is not only due to their 
dimensions, but also because the partial turns concept 
described in [8] could be implemented. This concept is based 
on having a full turn around a fraction of the total center leg 
flux. With an E-core shape, each outer leg has one-half of the 
total flux if the primary turns are wounded around the central 
leg, so a single turn around either outer leg will be equivalent 
to half a turn. Furthermore, according to [9], another benefit 
of this type of transformer is that the leakage inductance is 
decreased, and the thermal performance of the converter is 
improved by having the magnetic component distributed over 
several cores. Which gives rise to the main drawback of this 
type of transformer, the increased number of cores results in 
higher core losses.  
The resulting equivalent turns ratio, determined by the 
product of the number of primary windings and the number of 
cores, is not reflected by the individual core, but instead 
reflects the transformation ratio of the whole structure. In this 
particular case, since in the secondary-side it is implemented 
the partial winding, each of the E/PLT cores are actually two 
independent elements, so that “NCORES” parameter should be 
multiplied by a factor of two. Thus, achieving a higher turn 
ratio with a lower number of turns makes it possible to reduce 
the size of the converter and also the windings conduction 
losses. 
The PCB layout of the converter (Fig. 8) is made up of 
eight layers due to the fact that the converter is planar, so the 
interleaved winding of primary and secondary is integrated on 
the board. Moreover, being a converter for high-current 
applications, it is important to parallelize the secondary power 
layers in order to reduce conduction losses. 
 
Fig.  8.   PCB stack-up in eight layers for Planar Matrix Transformer.  
In conclusion, the transformer structure composed of a 
Planar Matrix Transformer with partial winding in the 
secondary-side can be advantageous for high-current 
applications with height limitations. The fabrication process 
can be automated, achieving higher power density solutions. 
Also, the termination connection between the transformer and 
the synchronous rectifiers can be reduced, leading to a 
significant decrease in conduction losses.

Using a matrix transformer composed of six individual 
cores will require balancing each of the magnetic cores. 
Having unbalanced cores introduces variable high frequency 
resonances on the secondary drain-source voltages as seen in 
Fig. 11. These oscillations negatively affect the performance 
of the converter, apart from the ringing which makes it 
impossible to have a high duty cycle due to the breaking 
voltage of the switches. 
 
Fig.  11.   Drain-Source voltage waveforms of secondary switches with 
unbalanced (right) and balanced (left) magnetic cores. 
The origin of the problem, which causes the resonances, is 
mainly related with the variations in the magnetizing 
inductances between different magnetic cores, which have an 
AL variation of ± 25% according to the manufacturer. 
Additionally, the magnetic flux generated by the primary turns 
around the central leg may not be distributed equally on each 
of the two outer legs of the core, resulting in higher dispersion 
inductance on one of the legs. 
In order to overcome the problem of having unbalanced 
cores, the solution implemented to decrease the resonances 
was to connect the drain electrical point of every switch 
between them. 
Tests on the power converter are performed for an input 
voltage of 48V with 1V at the output for a maximum current 
of 350A. The switching frequency is 150kHz and the duty 
cycle, limited by the breakdown voltage of the primary 
devices, was set around 75%. 
  
Different tests have been carried out to determine the 
performance of the power converter. The efficiency values are 
between 94% and 85% for an output current range up to 350A. 
Fig. 12 shows two efficiency curves; the black curve has 
unbalanced cores in the matrix structure, while the red one has 
all the cores balanced. This demonstrates the importance of 
having a proper arrangement of the cores, since having them 
unbalanced worsens the performance, especially at high load, 
as well as compromising the waveforms in secondary 
switches.  
 
Fig.  12.   Measured efficiency and losses for balanced and unbalanced 
power converter with air-cooling and without gate drive loss.

temperature reaches 45ºC. Under high-load conditions the 
scenario changes, as the 95ºC belong to the secondary

from Sanyo Denki of 67.8 CFM.

A. Low-Profile Direct Power Converter Optimized Version 
The latest version of the Low-Profile Direct Power 
Converter has been designed with the aim of making its 
package more compact, making better use of the available 
space to introduce secondary devices. In comparison with the 
previous version, the key changes are the duplication of 
secondary switches to forty-eight and the space for the 
implementation of the secondary turns has been optimized. 
Additionally, the concept of stacking is implemented in the 
primary-side. This minor change in the topology has a great 
advantage, the duty cycle can be significantly increased as it 
is less constrained by the voltage of the devices. 
 
Fig.  15.   3D model of the Low-Profile Direct Power Converter V2              
(83mm × 21mm × 5mm) 
Through theoretical simulations, the efficiency curve  
(Fig. 16) and the loss distribution graph (Fig. 17) have been 
obtained in order to evaluate the upgrades introduced in the 
Low-Profile Direct Power Converter V2 compared to the 
previous version. The latest version of the converter may 
represent a constant efficiency improvement of 3% for 
currents up to 200A. For higher currents, the difference 
between the versions is more remarkable, reaching a

V. CONCLUSION 
Increasing power consumption and higher losses due to 
bigger current demands in high-current applications, has 
forced the electronic sector to design power converters with 
higher power densities and higher efficiencies. The 48V-1V 
Low-Profile Direct Power Converter has been validated and 
demonstrated for an output current of 350A, using commercial 
cores and standard PCB. The results are good and promising, 
showing the potential of the converter for Power-on-Package 
applications, obtaining a peak efficiency of 94% and a full 
load efficiency of 85% with 412 W/in3 power density. A step 
forward is envisaged for the design of a low-profile power 
converter with high efficiency and high output current 
capability for high conversion gains. A 5mm-height power 
converter for high-current applications is feasible to be 
developed using the DPx topology, and adding modifications, 
such as the active clamp in primary-side and the Planar Matrix 
Transformer with the partial turns concept to reduce the height 
of the converter. Moreover, an improved version of the 
converter is designed, with compact packaging, in order to 
significantly reduce secondary conduction losses, achieving 
efficiency and power density values higher than the 48V-1V 
state-of-the-art power converters.
