with standard PCB transformer  
José A. Cobos, Pablo Mazariegos, Alejandro Figueroa, Alejandro Castro and Álvaro Cobos 
Differential Power S.L. 
28042 Madrid, Spain 
email: {jcobos, pablo.mazariegos, alex.figueroa, alejandro.castro, alvaro.cobos}@differentialpower.com 
Abstract— This article presents a high gain (24:1 V) high 
current (500A) dc-dc converter. One major advantage is the 
high efficiency measured, higher than 90% for 500A, higher 
than 95% for 200A, with a peak efficiency of 97.1%. Another 
major advantage is that the transformer is built using standard 
3oz PCB, which facilitates manufacturability and repeatability 
at a reduced cost. The prototype is built stacking two 12:1V 
modules, connecting primary sides in series and output sides in 
parallel with intrinsic voltage balance in the primary stack and 
intrinsic current sharing in the output capacitors. Stacking four 
modules to implement 48:1V is straight forward, maintaining 
the same efficiency. 
Keywords—Low-Profile Direct Power Converter, standard 
PCB, Planar-Matrix transformer, commercial cores. 
I. INTRODUCTION  
Artificial intelligence and high-performance chips require 
higher and higher currents (300 A – 30,000 A) in larger and 
larger chip area (300 – 30,000 mm2), hence “lateral” power 
delivery is no longer feasible due to voltage integrity and i2r 
losses. Power supplies are a challenge, and the key metric is 
A/mm2. The goal today with current PCB technology is > 
1A/mm2 with > 90% efficiency. 
The current density required by the chip must be matched 
by the current density generated by the power converter and 
injected directly in the chip. Today, overall efficiency in the 
power delivery path is low (e.g., 80%) and footprint is high 
(e.g., 70% of board real estate). The reason is the cascaded 
multiple power conversion stages, as shown in Fig. 1: 
- dc-dc converters to scale voltage and current 
- dc-dc converters to supply high di/dt to the chip 
Meeting all the combined specs in a single power 
conversion stage (direct to chip) in a flat form to be located 
underneath the chip (vertical power delivery) is the challenge 
power electronics community is pursuing [1-7]. 
The advantage of the “Direct to chip” power architecture 
is that there is only high current, hence high i2r losses in the 
very last mile, close to the chip.

POWER TOPOLOGY 
 
Fig.  2.   Topology of the 24V to 1V DPx-Matrix-Stacked converter 
The proposed converter is based on the “Direct Power 
Converter, DPx” [6-7], composed by a power transformer that 
is connected to input voltage and load by simultaneous 
conduction of the power switch in the primary side (“inverting 
power switch”) and the and the diode or synchronous rectifier 
in the secondary side. This “direct conduction state” enables a 
“direct power path” through the transformer for the input 
power to flow “directly” to the load. There is no alternate 
conduction of the inverting power switch and the diode (or 
synchronous rectifier) because converter operation is not 
based on energy “store and deliver” in inductors and 
capacitors as in standard SMPS. The limitation of this mode 
of operation is that the basic version of the circuit does not 
have the capability to regulate the gain of the converter. 
Circuit operation, described in [7], resembles in the 
primary side during the off time the resonance of a “Forward 
converter with Resonant Reset”. A Forward converter is the 
isolated version of a buck converter, which operation is based 
on alternatively storing and delivering energy in the buck 
inductor. Note that there is no such buck inductor in the 
secondary side of the DPx topology. 
The key advantage of the circuit is that the “direct 
conduction” interval may take place during a high duty cycle, 
D, hence reducing the rms value of the current flowing 
through both primary and secondary sides. 
Another apparent limitation of the circuit is the relatively 
high voltage in the power switches, which is intrinsically 
generated to demagnetize the transformer in the reset time, 1-
D. The value of D is a trade-off between the rms value of the 
current and the voltage in the semiconductors, which affect to 
their on-resistance. It should be noted that the AC power 
generated by the inverting power switch and rectified by the 
diode (synchronous rectifier) equals the power transferred by 
the transformer, that is, the output power. 
The AC power in the semiconductors defines their VA 
rating, and the choice of D determines the specific values of V 
and A. This means that the apparent limitation of the high

Although the basic version of the circuit does not require 
active clamps in primary or secondary sides, they may be used 
to reduce the voltage in the primary switches, applying a 
constant rather than sinusoidal demagnetization voltage. An 
additional advantage is that these voltages in primary and 
secondary side enable the control of the gain of the converter 
by commanding, within each switching cycle, the current that 
flows through the windings of the transformer, that is, the 
current injected into the output capacitor, isec. 
This capability to command the isec brings an important 
advantage: the capability to obtain a very high di/dt in the load. 
Novelty in this paper is also the demonstration of stacking 
in primary side as shown in Fig. 2, which enables 48:1V 
conversion by the stacking of 12V primary sides, which is a 
good trade-off given current GaN technology. The prototype 
is implemented using 200V devices with a nominal on-
resistance of 8 mΩ. 
Another significant step forward described in this paper is 
the implementation of the transformer in standard PCB, which 
enables manufacturability at reduced cost. Some further 
advantages of the topology are: 
• Efficiency: very high duty cycle (90%) in both primary 
and secondary switches (they conduct simultaneously) 
hence reducing rms current and conduction losses. 
• Load steps: transformer instead of Inductor → No Energy 
storage → 100x higher (10x smaller inductance, 10x 
higher voltage) di/dt injected to Cout.  
• Output voltage ripple: stacked primaries are phase 
shifted, reducing dramatically the current ripple injected to 
Cout, hence minimizing its value (6x), from 6mF to 1mF. 
Switching frequency: 5x lower frequency is used (200 
kHz) because the only benefit of increasing the frequency is 
core cross section, which is only 8mm2 because there is only 
1 turn and low voltage (1V) magnetizing the core. The MHz 
nightmare is gone. 
II. COMPARISON WITH OTHER TOPOLOGIES 
One of the reasons why “direct to chip” power conversion 
is challenging is because the power topologies used along the 
multiple conversion stages described in Fig. 1 cannot 
implement all the requirements of the whole power chain. 
• Hybrid converters: the combination of switched 
capacitors with inductances to enable soft charging of the 
capacitances has demonstrated excellent performance in 
low gain converters. They show excellent results in 48-
12V power stage, though their limitation is the high 
number of cascaded stages to achieve high gain, e.g., 48-
1V. 
• Transformer based converters: they are very appropriate 
to achieve the required high gain (48) of the application. 
Their penalty in most implementations as in LLC or 
“Current doubler rectifier, CDR” converter is that the 
rectification stage requires two rectifiers, which require 
twice the area than if only one rectifier were used, hence 
limiting the reduction of the on-resistance in the 
synchronous rectification stage. 
• Buck converters: they are typically used in the last 
conversion stage to supply the chip. Their limitation is the 
reduced input voltage, typically ranging from 1.8V to 6V. 
High input voltage as 48V would require higher values of 
the inductance, hence limiting their performance under 
load steps. 
For very high-performance chips, requiring very high 
current and very high di/dt, the comparison is based on the 
“rms factor”, the “synchronous rectifier filling factor” and the 
capability to supply “load steps”, as elaborated below.

B. Synchronous Rectifier Filling Factor 
The “synchronous rectifier factor”, SR_FF is defined as the 
ratio of the area occupied by the synchronous rectifiers and the 
available area for this purpose. 
A very high value may be obtained using the “Segmented 
Winding Transformer, SWT” as described in [7], connecting 
the synchronous rectifier and the output capacitor in opposite 
sides of the magnetic component, as illustrated in Fig. 4. This 
concept allows not only to maximize SR_FF but also enables 
integration in an interposer or even in the chip, beyond PCB.

C. Load steps  
The last requirement of “Direct to chip” topologies is the 
capability to supply high di/dt load steps.  
In a buck converter, the current injected to the output 
capacitor is limited by the output inductor, requiring high 
value of the output capacitor to supply the high di/dt required 
by the chip as illustrated in Fig. 5.a. 
For extreme di/dt, the current is supplied by the output 
capacitor, as illustrated in and Fig.5.b 
In a DPx converter, as illustrated in Fig. 5.c, both primary 
and secondary switches conduct simultaneously, enabling a 
“direct path” from input capacitor to output capacitor. This 
results in an effective parallel connection of the input 
capacitor with the output capacitor through the transformer, as 
illustrated in Fig. 4.d.

III. PROTOTYPE AND EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS 
The implemented converter shown in Fig. 8 takes 
advantage of the “Direct Power Converter, DPx” topology and 
the “Segmented Winding Transformer, SWT”, both described 
in [6-7]. Furthermore, the transformer is based on a matrix 
structure [8] which enables a very flat (6.5mm) 
implementation. 
A. Transformer Structure 
The transformer is built on a standard 3oz. PCB. Only 10 
layers are required to implement a a matrix transformer [8]. A 
very tight coupling is achieved by interleaving primary and 
secondary turns. The core is a custom extended EI core, 3C95 
ferrite. 
 
Fig.  8.   DPx prototype with Segmented Winding (SWT) in a Planar Matrix 
Transformer 
The secondary turns are implemented using the SWT 
concept to reduce i2r losses. The primary winding drives all 
the secondary cells with the same primary current, which 
balances all the paralleled secondary turns as shown in the 
thermal image in next section. 
The experimental results validated the theoretical 
calculations and simulations. Fig. 9 shows clean waveforms in 
the paralleled synchronous rectifiers. It should be noted that 
there is no imbalance in the waveforms thanks to the tight 
coupling among all the secondary windings with a single 
primary winding. 
 
 
 
Fig.  9.   Drain-Source waveforms in primary-side (top) and Secondary-side 
waveforms (bottom) (24V-1V @ 200A) 
B. Losses and Efficiency Structure 
Figure 10 shows the efficiency measured in the prototype. 
It matches very well with the calculations run in the 
optimization process. It should be noted that peak efficiency 
is >97% and efficiency is >90% at full load (500A). Efficiency 
at 200A is >94% being losses 2x lower than typical state-of-
the-art converters. 
Loss distribution is illustrated in Fig. 11. For different 
output currents. It should be noted that core losses are almost 
invariant, and losses at high current are mostly i2r losses.

IV. SPLIT POWER ARCHITECTURE 
The thermal image of the prototype operating at 500A 
shown in Fig. 12. is consistent with loss breakdown calculated 
in previous section. Losses are mostly i2r losses concentrated 
in the transformer and secondary side of the converter, that is, 
in the SWT.

Additionally, the PCB only requires 10 layers in the area 
dedicated to the SWT, but not in the area dedicated to primary 
switches. Note that there is an n ratio between primary and 
secondary currents and an n2 ratio between primary and 
secondary i2r losses. 
This fact suggested the idea of splitting primary and 
secondary sides of the power converter, as illustrated in Fig. 
13. 
The SWT may be located under the chip, to generate and 
supply high current to the chip. The primary switches and 
controller do not need to be located right under the chip, but 
somewhere else. 
This facilitates the modularity and scalability of the SWT, 
to be adapted to larger and larger chips. 
 
 
Fig.  13.   SPLIT architecture: “Segmented Winding Transformer, SWT” 
under the chip; primary switches and controller in a different location. 
Using this architecture, the specification for the current 
density of the chip is applied only to the SWT, which is the 
only part of the power converter located under the chip.

V. SUMMARY & CONCLUSIONS 
The measured 96% efficiency at 100A, 95% at 200A and 
90.5% at 500A is well beyond the state of the art. The 
converter is built on a 3oz standard PCB, with the transformer 
windings integrated in the PCB enabling manufacturability at 
reduced cost. 
The “Direct Power Converter, DPx” demonstrated 
superior performance for low voltage (1V) high current 
(500A) dc-dc power converters using copper bus bars [6] and 
now standard PCB transformer brings these concepts closer to 
actual products. “Vertical Power Delivery” may be 
implemented with standard PCB magnetic components, either 
in a traditional full module strategy or in a split architecture, 
placing only the “Segmented Winding Transformer, SWT” 
under the chip. 
Furthermore, the architecture is modular and scalable as 
illustrated in Fig. 15.
